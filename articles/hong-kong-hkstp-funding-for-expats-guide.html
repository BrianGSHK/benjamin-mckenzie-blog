How Expats Actually Use Hong Kong Government Funding: The HKSTP and ITF Guide

How an expat founder sets up a Hong Kong company, applies for HKSTP and Innovation and Technology Fund support, runs the business from a Hong Kong Island base, and books the subsidy correctly when it lands.

Benjamin McKenzie
Startup Mentor · Last reviewed 24 October 2026 · 16 min read

Scope and evidence: This is general editorial information, not legal, tax, accounting, audit or investment advice. Funding schemes, eligibility rules, ceilings and processing times change. Official sources and provider pages control — confirm the current position for your own facts with the Innovation and Technology Commission, the Inland Revenue Department or a qualified adviser before you commit money.

Founder tools: If you have not yet set up the entity, start with the Hong Kong company formation timeline and the non-resident registration guide before you read further.

Most expat founders get the Hong Kong funding question in the wrong order. They spend weeks researching which grant they could win, and then discover that the grant requires a Hong Kong-incorporated company with a bank account, real premises and clean books — none of which exist yet. The order that actually works is: company first, operations second, application third, and the tax treatment of the money in your favour understood before you spend the first dollar.

Having watched this play out repeatedly over ten years of mentoring Hong Kong founders — including expats who arrived with no local network, no local credit history and no idea where to file — the pattern is consistent.

The founders who succeed treat government funding as a cash-flow and evidence problem, not a grant-scavenging exercise. The ones who fail apply to schemes that do not match their business, or win a grant and then discover the subsidy was taxable all along.

Key Takeaway: The Government of Hong Kong funds technology businesses, not founders. Your company must be incorporated in Hong Kong, must have a current business registration certificate, and must be able to evidence real operations — before any application is filed. Once a grant is approved, treat the money as taxable income unless the Government has expressly stated otherwise, because section 15(1)(c) of the Inland Revenue Ordinance deems grants and subsidies to be trading receipts.

Direct Answers

What government funding can an expat founder in Hong Kong actually apply for?

The money is the ITF, not HKSTP. Through the Innovation and Technology Fund, administered by the Innovation and Technology Commission (ITC), the main routes for a Hong Kong-incorporated technology company are the Enterprise Support Scheme (ESS) for in-house R&D, the Public Sector Trial Scheme for prototypes and government trials, the Research Talent Hub for recruiting research staff, and the General Support Programme for non-R&D industry activity. If you are a Hong Kong Science and Technology Parks (HKSTP) incubatee, the Incubation Programme and the Public Sector Trial Scheme for Incubatees and Graduate Tenants (PSTS-SPC) sit on top of that. You apply as a company, and the company must already exist.

Is a Hong Kong government grant tax-free?

Generally, no — grants are taxable. Section 15(1)(c) of the Inland Revenue Ordinance provides that sums received by or accrued to a person carrying on business in Hong Kong by way of grant, subsidy or similar financial assistance, other than sums in connection with capital expenditure, are deemed receipts arising in or derived from Hong Kong from a trade, profession or business. Unless the Government states that the specific grant is not subject to profits tax, the subsidy is taxable. A grant connected with capital expenditure sits outside that deeming provision.

Do I need to live on Hong Kong Island to run a Hong Kong company and win funding?

No, and the address is not the test. Funding eligibility is about the company, not your postal address. A registered address satisfies the Companies Ordinance, but it does not evidence substantive operations, and reviewers assess commercial viability.

Hong Kong Island is however the most convenient place to put your working base in practice: the corporate service providers, the banks, the accountants and the HKSTP ecosystem are all reachable from one district, so your registered address, bank relationship and operating presence can all point to the same place. For an expat, that single-district approach is the lowest-friction way to build an evidence trail that holds up to scrutiny.

How long does it take to get funding approved and paid?

About two months to a vetting result, then three more. For the Enterprise Support Scheme, the ITC states an applicant can normally expect to be notified of the vetting result in around two months after submitting all required information and documents. If recommended for funding, you have a further three months to address the assessment panel's comments on scope, budget and milestones before final approval and the fund agreement. Payment itself is milestone-based against the approved project budget, not on approval, so you must fund the project cash flow yourself first.

Start with the company, because no scheme lets you skip it

Every ITF scheme I have worked with starts from the same three eligibility conditions, and they are non-negotiable. Your company must be incorporated in Hong Kong through the Companies Registry, must hold a current business registration certificate, and must not be a government subvented organisation or a subsidiary of one. There is no version of this where an overseas entity, a personal application, or a partnership in place of a limited company works.

This is why the sequence matters. If you spend three months choosing between GetStarted.hk and Monx, and then another three months waiting for a grant, you have spent half a year in the wrong order.

The realistic path for an expat founder — one that I have walked founders down many times — looks like this:

  1. Incorporate the Hong Kong company and open a corporate bank account. This is the gating step. No scheme application can be filed without the incorporation documents, the business registration certificate and the latest annual return.
  2. Create the evidence of real operations. A premises address, a bank account with activity, staff or contracted local capability, and a business plan that shows how the project will be executed in Hong Kong.
  3. Identify the right scheme — most commonly ESS for R&D — and confirm the matching ratio and eligible cost scope before you write a budget.
  4. Prepare the application with the supporting documents the scheme requires, including the organisation chart used to prove the eligibility criteria. If you are not yet sure which scheme fits, the HKSTP TecONE advisory desk and the HKSTP Ideation Programme are the two free public entry points.
  5. Run the project on clean books and claim reimbursement against milestones, understanding that the subsidy is taxable income in the year it becomes receivable.

Why this matters: A grant you cannot evidence your Hong Kong operations for is a grant you will have to repay, with the reporting obligations attached. Building the evidence trail before you apply costs a few months of setup. Building it after a failed assessment costs you the funding cycle and the reputation of the programme.

Pick the scheme that matches what you are actually building

The Innovation and Technology Commission (ITC) publishes the eligibility conditions for each route, and the commonest filing error I see is not a weak application — it is a strong application for the wrong scheme. When the money arrives, the same company will need audited accounts and a defensible tax position on the grant, and the Deloitte audit and assurance practice in Hong Kong and China and KPMG's Hong Kong SAR audit service are where founders look for that standard of work. A founder with a working prototype and a signed pilot with a government bureau applies for ESS to fund more R&D, when the money they need is trial funding.

Another founder with genuine in-house R&D applies for a general brand-building grant, which cannot fund engineering salaries. Match the instrument to the activity.

ESS deserves a second look because it is the scheme most first-time applicants can actually use. It is open all year round, and its five assessment criteria are weighted and published on the official ESS programme page: commercial viability of project deliverables at 30%, innovation and technology at 25%, technical and management capability at 20%, reasonableness of project cost at 15%, and relevance to the overall interest of the community or government policies at 10%.

An application must pass each criterion. That published weighting is genuinely useful to a first-time applicant, because it tells you what the panel is reading your document for — and a project with no route to market fails the criterion that carries the most weight.

File the application properly, because the paperwork is part of the assessment

ESS asks for a signed and stamped original hard copy submitted through the Innovation and Technology Commission Funding Administrative System (ITCFAS), the online system run by the Innovation and Technology Commission (ITC), and the supporting documents are specific. You need the Certificate of Incorporation, a valid Business Registration Certificate from the Companies Registry, the latest annual return — or the incorporation form for a newly established company —

any notices of change of company secretary and director, any notice or certificate of change of company name, and an organisation chart of your company, and of your company group if you have one, as proof that you meet the eligibility criteria.

That last item is where non-resident founders get caught out. The organisation chart is not a formality. It is how the Secretariat verifies that you are not a government subvented organisation or a subsidiary of one, and it is how the panel reads your technical and management capability.

Draw it properly, show which entity employs your R&D staff, and make sure the company secretary and directors in it match your filings exactly.

A procurement detail that trips people up: From 1 January 2026 the upper quotation limit for procurement of goods and services in an ESS application was revised from HK$1,360,000 to HK$1,350,000, applying to applications first submitted through the ITCFAS on or after that date.

If you are preparing quotations in October 2026, the HK$1,350,000 threshold is the one that applies to you. It is a small number on paper and a rejected application in practice, because the panel treats procurement compliance as part of reasonableness of project cost.

Make Hong Kong Island your working base, because it is the cheapest way to be legible

Let me be direct about this, because I think the instinct is usually right even when the reasoning is muddled. Founders often choose Hong Kong Island because it is where the good coffee is and the offices are pleasant, and then struggle to connect that to funding. The connection is real, and it is about evidence.

Think about what a panel is trying to establish when they read "reasonableness of project cost" and "commercial viability of project deliverables". They are asking: is this project real, is it happening here, and can this team actually execute it?

The evidence that answers that question is a bank account with activity, a lease or desk you can point to, a team that is employed or contracted, and a set of books that tie together. Every one of those pieces of evidence needs to be somewhere, and every one of them needs to be reachable by a reviewer or a auditor.

Putting your registered address, your bank relationship, your accounting and your service providers in the same compact district on Hong Kong Island is the most efficient way to make that evidence internally consistent.

It is not that Island addresses score more points. It is that a coherent, verifiable local presence is the thing that wins applications, and one district is the cheapest way to build one.

For an expat who does not yet live in Hong Kong, the practical route is a serviced office or a dedicated coworking desk in a business district with a mail-handling address, a registered business address that satisfies the Companies Ordinance, and a business bank account in the same place.

Combine that with a locally employed or contracted operations lead, and you have the skeleton of an evidence trail. For expats who do plan to live on the island, the housing choice and the business base can be made in the same district — and that is the practical, low-friction version of "live where you work".

One caution on immigration. Working in Hong Kong requires permission, whatever your nationality.

The Admission Scheme for Mainland Talents and Professionals (ASMTP) is quota-free and non-sector specific, and it normally grants an initial stay of 36 months on employment condition, with an extension route for professionals who have held that status for at least two years and have assessable salaries tax income of not less than HK$2 million in the previous year of assessment.

The Top Talent Admission Scheme (TechTAS), the General Employment Policy (GEP), the Quality Migrant Admission Scheme and other routes apply to other profiles. If you are not a Hong Kong resident and you intend to be on the ground, this is a separate workstream from funding, and it is better to run it in parallel than to discover it late. For a founder already admitted under a scheme, note that professionals admitted under the ASMTP who have ordinarily resided in Hong Kong for seven continuous years may apply for the right of abode.

Book the subsidy as taxable income unless the Government says otherwise

This is the section that costs the most money when founders get it wrong, and it is the one I most want you to read carefully. Hong Kong has no general "grant is tax-free" rule. The default is the opposite. The PwC Hong Kong tax practice and EY's tax services in Greater China both publish current Hong Kong tax commentary, which is the quickest way to sanity-check the treatment before your accountant does.

Section 15(1)(c) of the Inland Revenue Ordinance provides that sums received by or accrued to a person carrying on business in Hong Kong by way of grant, subsidy or similar financial assistance, other than sums in connection with capital expenditure, are deemed to be receipts arising in or derived from Hong Kong from a trade, profession or business. In plain terms: the money a government grant puts into your company is treated as trading income, and the money you spend on the approved project is a deductible expense.

The grant is not a tax-free injection. It is income, with costs against it.

There are two exceptions you should know about and neither is automatic. First, a sum received in connection with capital expenditure falls outside the deeming provision — so a grant that funds the acquisition of a qualifying asset is treated differently from a grant that funds your engineers' salaries.

Second, a specific grant may be exempt where the Government expressly says so, and that is the only reliable source of an exemption.

Do not assume a scheme is exempt because the public money "isn't really income". Read the scheme's funding and administrative guidelines, and if they are silent, take the taxable treatment. The IRD's Departmental Interpretation and Practice Notes explain how the Department applies the Ordinance in practice, and they are the right place to look before you argue a position.

Under HKAS 20, the recognition principle is straightforward and it is worth internalising rather than memorising. A government grant is recognised in profit or loss when the company becomes entitled to receive it, unless the grant is related to the acquisition of an asset, in which case it is recognised as a deferred credit and released systematically to profit or loss over the useful life of the asset.

Grants that are not refundable are recognised only when the company becomes entitled to receive them, and a refundable grant is recognised when it becomes receivable or when the related costs are incurred, whichever is earlier.

Now the part where founders make an expensive error, so let me be blunt. Accounting and tax are two separate questions here, and the accounting answer does not decide the tax answer.

HKAS 20 tells you how to present the grant in the profit and loss account. Section 15(1)(c) tells you whether that income is taxable in Hong Kong. A deferred credit presentation under HKAS 20 does not defer your tax charge, and a grant that is taxable does not become non-taxable because you presented it carefully.

When you present a grant as a deferred credit, the tax treatment still follows the receipt and accrual rules. Get your accountant to write down both answers side by side in the file before you sign the fund agreement — not after the first assessment arrives.

Keep the grant in a separate ledger. Grant-funded spend must be traceable to approved project costs, because reimbursement claims are checked line by line and the audit is not a formality. A clean project-cost ledger that maps to the approved budget is the single artefact that protects you if the claim is challenged, and it is much harder to assemble retrospectively than to build from day one.

Check what the evidence establishes before you rely on it

It is worth being precise about the boundary, because funding applications are high-stakes documents and the rules move.

  • Confirmed by the sources cited: scheme eligibility conditions, funding ceilings, matching ratios, project duration limits, the published ESS assessment weightings, the HK$1,350,000 procurement quotation limit applying from 1 January 2026, the operation of section 15(1)(c) on grants and subsidies, and the 2026 BUD Fund ceilings and payment structure.
  • Provider-published, not independently established: what GetStarted.hk and Monx publish about their own services, their pricing, their client counts and their stated capabilities. Provider pages establish what a provider publishes, not a guaranteed outcome.
  • Fact-dependent and not determinable in advance: whether your application succeeds, whether a bank opens your account, whether the panel accepts your project cost, whether the Inland Revenue Department accepts your characterisation of a specific grant, and whether a particular grant is exempt. These turn on your facts and on the evidence you assemble.

Nothing in this article is a promise of funding, a guarantee of a grant's tax treatment, or an assurance that a particular company structure will survive an Inland Revenue Department review. The schemes are competitive and the budgets are finite. What you can control is whether your company, your operations and your books are ready to carry the application.

Funding readiness checklist

  1. Confirm your company is incorporated in Hong Kong with a current business registration certificate, and that you are not a government subvented organisation or a subsidiary of one.
  2. Draw the organisation chart, including the company group if you have one, and check it against your filings line by line.
  3. Identify the single scheme that matches your activity, and read its funding and administrative guidelines rather than a summary of it.
  4. Confirm the matching ratio and the eligible cost scope, then build a cash-flow plan that survives reimbursement-based payment.
  5. Set up a project-cost ledger before the first project invoice, mapped to the approved budget headings.
  6. Get a written tax position on the specific grant, distinguishing revenue grants from capital-expenditure grants.
  7. Assemble the Hong Kong operations evidence: bank account activity, premises, local team or contracted capability, and clean books from incorporation.

A worked subsidy tax example

Assume a company receives an ESS grant of HK$1,000,000 in the year of assessment 2026/27 to fund two engineers' salaries and consumables totalling HK$1,000,000. Because the sum is received by way of grant and is not connected with capital expenditure, it is a deemed trading receipt under section 15(1)(c) and is included in assessable profits for 2026/27. The HK$1,000,000 of eligible expenditure is deductible under section 16 of the Ordinance to the extent incurred in the production of chargeable profits. On a standalone basis, with no other income, the company has a nil net assessable profit for the year and no profits tax is payable — but the grant has still been reported as income, the expenditure has still been claimed, and the records must support both positions if the account is later audited.

If the same grant were instead received in connection with capital expenditure, it would fall outside the section 15(1)(c) deeming provision, and the accounting presentation would follow the asset-related route in HKAS 20.

A second example: the same grant, a different tax answer

Now change one fact. The company receives HK$1,000,000 in the same year, but the grant funds a new piece of production equipment costing HK$1,000,000. Three things change at once, and founders miss the second and third.

  • The tax position changes. The sum is received in connection with capital expenditure, so it sits outside the section 15(1)(c) deeming provision. The company is not required to treat it as a trading receipt.
  • The accounting position changes. Under HKAS 20 the grant is not recognised in profit or loss in full. It is recognised as a deferred credit and released to profit or loss over the useful life of the asset. Assuming a five-year useful life, HK$200,000 would be released in 2026/27 and HK$200,000 in each of the four following years.
  • The tax position does not simply follow the accounting position. The deferred credit presentation does not create a tax deduction and does not defer an assessment. Whether the expenditure gives rise to an allowable deduction, and when, is a separate question under section 16 of the Ordinance and the capital expenditure rules, and it is a question for your accountant rather than a consequence of the accounting standard.

The practical point: the same HK$1,000,000 of government money can produce a taxable receipt in year one or a deferred credit spread over five years, depending on what it funds.

And a deferral in the accounts is not a deferral of tax. If your company is at the 8.25% rate on its first HK$2 million of profits and a subsidy of HK$1,000,000 is the only income that year, the difference between treating it as trading income and treating it as capital-related can be HK$82,500 of tax in the year of receipt. That is a large enough number to justify a specific written tax position before you accept the grant, and it is exactly the conversation I would have with a client before signing a fund agreement.

Read the frequently asked questions

Can an expat founder apply for HKSTP funding?

Yes, but the company applies, not the founder. The funding is never for you personally — it is for the Hong Kong company. Every scheme runs through the Innovation and Technology Commission and requires the applicant to be incorporated in Hong Kong with a current business registration certificate. A founder can be a non-Hong Kong resident and still apply, but you need the company first, plus real Hong Kong operations to evidence.

What is the difference between HKSTP and ITF funding?

HKSTP delivers the programmes; the ITF holds the money. HKSTP is the delivery and incubation layer — it runs the Ideation and Incubation programmes, hosts TecONE as the free public-funding advisory desk, and routes you to the right scheme. The Innovation and Technology Fund (ITF) is the money itself, administered by the Innovation and Technology Commission. HKSTP helps you apply; the ITC decides and pays.

Which scheme should a small hardware or software startup apply for first?

ESS for in-house R&D; PSTS-SPC for government pilots. The Enterprise Support Scheme (ESS) is the workhorse for in-house R&D: dollar-for-dollar matching, up to HK$10 million per approved project over up to 24 months, with the company keeping the IP. If you are an HKSTP incubatee trying to sell into government, the Public Sector Trial Scheme for Incubatees and Graduate Tenants (PSTS-SPC) funds prototype and trial work up to HK$1 million per project.

How long does an ESS application take?

About two months to a vetting result, then three more to close. The ITC states that an applicant can normally expect to be notified of the vetting result in around two months after submitting all required information and documents. If the assessment panel recommends funding, you then have three months to revise the proposal and address panel comments before the fund agreement is signed. Treat the two months as the decision window, not the total timeline.

Do I need to be physically in Hong Kong to hold an office on Hong Kong Island?

No — but the address has to be evidenced. A Hong Kong Island address is not required by the funding schemes, and a registered address satisfies the Companies Ordinance without evidencing real operations. What matters is that whatever address you use can be evidenced, and that grants are paid into a bank account that shows real activity. Many founders use a serviced office or coworking desk on Hong Kong Island as the cheapest credible starting point.

Are HKSTP and ITF grants tax-free in Hong Kong?

No — grants are taxable unless the Government exempts them. This is the most expensive misunderstanding in this area. Under section 15(1)(c) of the Inland Revenue Ordinance, sums received by way of grant, subsidy or similar financial assistance are deemed trading receipts arising in or derived from Hong Kong, other than sums received in connection with capital expenditure. Unless the Government itself states the grant is not subject to profits tax, treat the subsidy as taxable income in the year of assessment in which it is receivable. KPMG's Hong Kong SAR tax insights track the same point from a Hong Kong practice perspective.

How should a grant be recorded in the accounts?

Recognise it in profit or loss when the company becomes entitled to receive it. Under HKAS 20, a grant related to the acquisition of an asset is instead recognised as a deferred credit and released to profit or loss over the useful life of that asset. The cash and the tax treatment are separate questions: the accounting standard governs the profit-and-loss presentation, and the Inland Revenue Ordinance governs whether that profit is taxable in Hong Kong. If the company has not yet filed, our guide to your first Hong Kong profits tax return sets out the filing sequence.

Does the 1:3 matching ratio apply to every scheme?

No — every ratio is scheme-specific. BUD Fund is 1:3 government-to-company, meaning the company funds at least 75% of approved project cost in cash. ESS is dollar-for-dollar matching. The New Industrialisation Acceleration Scheme (NIAS) is 1 government to 2 enterprise. Read the specific scheme's funding and administrative guidelines before you build a budget assumption around any ratio.

Do I need a Hong Kong resident director or an MPF-enrolled employee to win a grant?

No resident director is required, but real operations are what reviewers look for. For the grant itself, you need incorporation in Hong Kong, a business registration certificate, and an organisation chart. There is no general resident-director requirement. But most schemes are assessed on commercial viability and evidence of local operations, and a local bank account, an MPF-enrolled employee and real premises are the evidence that reviewers look for. A nominee director and a registered address alone will not carry an application.

What does it cost to set up before the first grant payment arrives?

Budget the company first, then the working-capital gap. You cannot apply without the company. Incorporation plus a year of company secretary, registered address and accounting typically runs HK$5,000 to HK$15,000 for the first year, and the company will also need a corporate bank account, which is where most non-resident founders hit friction. Our guide to opening a Hong Kong bank account as a foreigner covers that step. Working capital must cover the gap between spending on the project and receiving the grant.

Is GetStarted.hk or Monx better for an expat founder applying for funding?

Compare the funding support, not the formation price. Both are Hong Kong corporate service providers and both support the formation, accounting and tax work you need either way. The practical difference for a funding applicant is whether the provider will actually prepare and support a funding application and an audited project budget, and whether they will make the Hong Kong operations evidence real rather than nominal. Compare the scope of the funding-support work, not just the incorporation price.

Do I need to live in Hong Kong to keep the company eligible for funding?

The company must operate in Hong Kong; you need not live there. What you need is a company that is genuinely operating in Hong Kong, not necessarily to live in Hong Kong yourself. But if you are a non-resident founder working with a Hong Kong team, a Hong Kong employee or a contracted local operations lead, then the practical centre of gravity of the business is on the island, and running the registered address, bank account and service providers in the same district is the simplest way to keep the evidence consistent.
