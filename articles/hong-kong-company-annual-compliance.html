Hong Kong Company Annual Compliance: What You Need to Know

A complete guide to keeping your Hong Kong company in good standing, covering annual returns, audits, tax filing, and deadlines.

Benjamin McKenzie
Aug 11, 2026 · 7 min read

For a practical view of how corporate account applications may be processed, see our anonymised Hong Kong corporate banking research report.

Quick Answer: Hong Kong annual compliance is a connected system rather than one annual form. A company normally needs to file its Annual Return, maintain accounting records, renew its Business Registration Certificate and deal with audited accounts and Profits Tax Returns where applicable. Exact duties and dates depend on the company’s status, accounting period and IRD notices.

Direct Answers

What are the main annual compliance requirements for a Hong Kong company?

A Hong Kong company generally needs to keep proper accounting records, file its Annual Return with the Companies Registry, renew its Business Registration Certificate and address its Profits Tax obligations. An active company will usually also prepare audited financial statements. Dormant status or another statutory exception may change one obligation, but not automatically remove every filing.

When must a private company file its Annual Return?

A private company normally files its Annual Return within 42 days after the anniversary of incorporation. The return updates the Companies Registry on matters such as the registered office, directors, company secretary, shareholders and share capital. The filing deadline is separate from the company’s financial year-end, so founders should track both dates.

Do all Hong Kong companies need audited accounts?

Not every company has identical obligations. An active Hong Kong company generally prepares annual financial statements and has them audited, while a qualifying dormant private company may use the relevant statutory exemption. The company should confirm its status and filing position for the year; “no sales” or “no bank movement” does not automatically establish dormancy.

What should a founder track each year?

Use one compliance calendar with four separate anchors: the incorporation anniversary, the Business Registration Certificate expiry date, the financial year-end and the response date on the IRD Profits Tax Return. Add time for bookkeeping, audit, review, signatures and corrections. A calendar works best when each deadline has an owner and a document checklist.

How does Annual Return filing work?

The Annual Return is a snapshot of the company’s particulars at the relevant anniversary date. It is not a tax return and it does not replace annual accounts or the Profits Tax Return. Before filing, check the company name and number, registered office, directors, company secretary, shareholders, share capital and any registrable charges.

The Companies Registry’s private-company guidance explains the filing requirement and fees. The Companies Ordinance reference provides the statutory context. Use the current official fee rather than carrying forward an amount from an older checklist.

What happens if the Annual Return is late?

Late filing can lead to higher statutory fees and, in serious or persistent cases, enforcement or prosecution risk. The exact amount depends on the type of company and the length of the delay. If a return is overdue, file it promptly, check the current fee and do not assume that a dormant or inactive label removes the obligation.

How should a company renew its Business Registration Certificate?

The Business Registration Certificate is administered by the Inland Revenue Department. Check the certificate’s expiry date and follow the IRD Business Registration renewal process. Fees and levies can change, so confirm the amount for the relevant period rather than relying on an old article or spreadsheet.

Keep the renewed certificate with the company’s statutory and accounting records. If the company changes its address, business activity or responsible people, review whether other notifications or updates are also needed. Business Registration renewal is separate from the Companies Registry Annual Return and should have its own calendar reminder.

When are accounts and audit required?

Start with the company’s actual status, not a slogan such as “zero activity”. An active company should maintain proper books, prepare financial statements and arrange an audit where required. A qualifying dormant private company may be able to rely on the relevant Companies Ordinance exemption, but it must still review Annual Return, Business Registration and tax obligations separately.

The Companies Registry dormant-company FAQ and the IRD dormant-company FAQ should be read together with the company’s records. An accounting transaction may affect dormant status. Keep bank statements, invoices, professional-fee records and other evidence that supports the position taken for the relevant period.

What should the audit file contain?

  • Bank statements and reconciliations for every account.
  • Sales, purchase and expense records, including payment-platform exports where relevant.
  • Supplier invoices issued to the correct legal entity.
  • Asset, loan, shareholder and director-balance schedules.
  • Contracts and explanations for unusual or high-value transactions.
  • Prior-year accounts, audit reports and tax computations.

How should founders handle the Profits Tax Return?

A Profits Tax Return is not the same document as the Annual Return. The IRD notice sets the relevant response date and filing requirements. For a corporation, BIR51 is the corporate return, but supplementary forms and schedules depend on the taxpayer, transactions, tax regime, filing mode and year of assessment. Check the IRD Profits Tax guidance and current completion material.

Prepare the tax file from the accounts rather than starting with a blank form. Reconcile revenue and costs to bank statements, review tax adjustments, identify related-party transactions and preserve evidence for material deductions. If an offshore position is being considered, analyse the source of each profit stream separately; incorporation, a Hong Kong bank account or an overseas customer is not conclusive by itself. Our offshore versus onshore guide explains that distinction.

What should happen before submitting the return?

  • Confirm the company, year of assessment and accounting period.
  • Reconcile bank accounts, merchant gateways and payment platforms.
  • Check that invoices and contracts identify the correct legal entity.
  • Review whether supplementary forms apply.
  • Separate accounting profit from tax-adjusted assessable profit.
  • Allow time for audit, review, signing and submission.

What happens when a company misses a compliance deadline?

Act on the first reminder rather than waiting for the problem to become a prosecution matter. Depending on the obligation and facts, consequences may include late fees, penalty demands, estimated assessment, a compound offer, summons or recovery action. The IRD penalty policy and the government’s tax-recovery guidance provide useful official context.

Do not assume that paying a late fee resolves every outstanding duty. Identify the missed filing, submit the correct document, respond to correspondence and obtain advice if there is an estimated assessment, disputed tax, missing evidence or possible director liability.

How can a founder build a simple compliance system?

Use four folders for each year: statutory records, accounting records, tax records and correspondence. At the start of the year, record the anniversary and certificate dates. Each month, save bank and platform records. Before year-end, close the books and identify missing invoices. Before submission, complete the audit and tax review together. After filing, save the final package and proof of submission.

For supplementary professional commentary on filing-season developments, founders may review KPMG’s Hong Kong Profits Tax filing guide and EY’s 2025/26 filing-season alert. These are professional commentary, not substitutes for the IRD’s notices or statutory guidance.
