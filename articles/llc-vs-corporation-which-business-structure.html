LLC vs Corporation: Which Business Structure Should You Choose?

A practical comparison of LLCs and corporations covering liability, taxes, compliance, and costs to help you make the right decision.

BM
Benjamin McKenzie
Aug 11, 2026 · 7 min read

An LLC offers flexible management and pass-through taxation, while a corporation provides a formal governance structure and potential for raising capital. Both structures protect your personal assets from business liabilities, but they differ significantly in taxes, compliance, and operational flexibility.

Quick Answer: Choose an LLC for simplicity, flexibility, and pass-through taxation. Choose a corporation if you plan to raise venture capital, issue stock options, or go public. Many startups begin as LLCs and convert to corporations when seeking investment.

What Is an LLC?

A Limited Liability Company (LLC) is a business structure that combines the liability protection of a corporation with the tax flexibility of a partnership. LLCs are available in most countries and are particularly popular in the United States.

Key Characteristics of an LLC:

  • Limited liability protection — Personal assets are protected from business debts and lawsuits
  • Pass-through taxation — Business income flows to owners' personal tax returns
  • Flexible management — No requirement for formal board meetings or corporate minutes
  • Ownership flexibility — Can have unlimited members (individuals or other businesses)
  • Less paperwork — Fewer ongoing compliance requirements than corporations

What Is a Corporation?

A corporation is a legal entity that is separate and distinct from its owners. Corporations can be C-corporations (default) or S-corporations (tax election). They are required to follow strict governance and reporting rules.

Key Characteristics of a Corporation:

  • Separate legal entity — The corporation itself can own assets, enter contracts, and be sued
  • Double taxation — Corporate profits taxed at corporate rate, then dividends taxed again at individual rate (C-corp only)
  • Formal structure — Requires board of directors, officers, and annual meetings
  • Stock issuance — Can issue different classes of stock to raise capital
  • Perpetual existence — Continues regardless of ownership changes

Tax Implications

The tax treatment is often the deciding factor when choosing between an LLC and a corporation.

LLC Taxation

By default, LLCs are pass-through entities. The business itself does not pay federal income taxes. Instead, profits and losses flow through to members' personal tax returns. This avoids the double taxation problem that C-corporations face.

Single-member LLCs report income on Schedule C of the owner's Form 1040. Multi-member LLCs file Form 1065 (partnership return) and issue K-1s to members.

Corporation Taxation

C-corporations pay corporate income tax on their profits (currently 21% federal rate in the US). When dividends are distributed to shareholders, those dividends are taxed again at the individual level — this is called double taxation.

S-corporations avoid double taxation by passing income through to shareholders, similar to LLCs. However, S-corps have stricter ownership rules (100 shareholders maximum, US residents only, one class of stock).

Example: A business earns $100,000 in profit. As an LLC, the owner pays personal income tax on $100,000. As a C-corp, the company pays $21,000 corporate tax, and the remaining $79,000 distributed as dividends is taxed again at 15-20%.

Compliance Requirements

Corporations face significantly more compliance requirements than LLCs:

LLC Compliance

  • File annual report with the state (most states)
  • Pay annual franchise tax or fee (varies by state)
  • Maintain registered agent in the state of formation
  • Keep basic financial records

Corporation Compliance

  • Hold annual shareholder meetings
  • Hold regular board meetings
  • Document meeting minutes and resolutions
  • File annual reports with the state
  • Maintain corporate bylaws
  • Issue stock certificates
  • File separate corporate tax returns

When to Choose an LLC

An LLC is typically the better choice when:

  • You are a small business owner or freelancer
  • You want simplicity and minimal paperwork
  • You prefer flexible management without formal board requirements
  • You want pass-through taxation to avoid double taxation
  • You are not planning to raise venture capital or go public
  • You have a single owner or small number of owners

When to Choose a Corporation

A corporation is typically the better choice when:

  • You plan to raise venture capital or seek outside investment
  • You want to issue stock options to employees
  • You plan to go public (IPO) in the future
  • You need to establish credibility with large corporate clients
  • You want to attract investors who expect a C-corp structure
  • You are building a scalable startup

Pro Tip: Many successful startups begin as LLCs and convert to C-corporations when they are ready to raise venture capital. This approach lets you save on compliance costs during the early stages while maintaining flexibility.

Can You Change Your Structure Later?

Yes. You can convert an LLC to a corporation (or vice versa) if your business needs change. The process varies by state but generally involves filing conversion documents and updating your operating agreement or bylaws.

However, conversions can trigger tax events, so consult with a tax professional before making the switch.
