Hong Kong IP Tax Deduction Bill and 2025/26 Return Deadlines: What Founders Need to Know

The IP expenditure deduction Bill gazetted on 16 October 2026, the real 2025/26 return deadlines from the March block extension circular, and six new double taxation agreements - what each one actually requires of a Hong Kong company.

Filing deadlines, tax rules and treaty positions change, and the Inland Revenue Department assesses every taxpayer on the facts of the year of assessment. Two Hong Kong tax announcements landed in the same fortnight, and they are not the same kind of news. One is forward-looking and unpassed: the Inland Revenue (Amendment) (Tax Deductions for Intellectual Property Expenditure) Bill 2026, gazetted on 16 October 2026, which would let you deduct more of the cost of buying and licensing intellectual property.

The other is a filing calendar that has been running since April 2026: the extended deadlines for 2025/26 returns, set out in a Circular Letter the Commissioner of Inland Revenue issued to tax representatives on 19 March 2026.

Confusing the two produces two specific errors, and I have seen both in client conversations this month. The first is believing that a filing deadline moved last week. It did not.

The second is working from a set of dates that the Department never published, which is worse, because a wrong date on a compliance calendar does not announce itself until the penalty arrives.

Having mentored Hong Kong founders for over a decade, and sat with tax practitioners who file hundreds of these returns every year, the pattern is consistent: the risk is not in the legislation, it is in the dates.

The Bill is easy to summarise. The lodgement calendar has four routes, three deadlines and a condition attached to the most generous one, and it has to be applied to the right accounting date code.

Direct Answers

What is the IP tax deduction Bill 2026, and when does it apply?

It is a Bill that would let you deduct more of the cost of buying and licensing intellectual property, and it applies to contracts entered into on or after 1 April 2026. The Inland Revenue (Amendment) (Tax Deductions for Intellectual Property Expenditure) Bill 2026 was gazetted on 16 October 2026, and its First and Second Reading were scheduled for 28 October 2026.

It proposes three deductions: buying eligible IP rights from associates, buying eligible IP rights that a licensee outside Hong Kong uses, and paying an upfront licence fee. It is a proposal, not law, and it does not change what you must file for 2025/26.

What is the deadline for my 2025/26 return?

For an M code accounting date, which means an accounting date between 1 January 2026 and 31 March 2026, the paper filing deadline is 16 November 2026. You can get one further month on application if you e-file voluntarily, and if your company sustained an allowable loss you can reach 1 February 2027, but the list of departmental file numbers must reach the Inland Revenue Department by 2 November 2026.

There is no 30 November or 30 December deadline, and the scheme itself is not new: the Circular Letter setting it out was issued on 19 March 2026.

Which six double taxation agreements are new, and are they in force?

Barbados, Jordan, the Kyrgyz Republic, Maldives, Norway and Rwanda. The Chief Executive in Council made the Orders on 6 October 2026, they were gazetted on 16 October 2026, and they were tabled at the Legislative Council for negative vetting on 21 October 2026.

None of them is in force yet: each enters into force only after both Hong Kong and the other jurisdiction have completed their respective ratification procedures. If you invoice a customer in one of these six countries, do not assume treaty relief is available today.

What should you do first?

Ask your tax representative which accounting date code applies to your company and what the real extended due date is, because a wrong date on a compliance calendar does not announce itself until the penalty arrives.

If you are a loss-making company with an M code, start the loss-case extension application now, because 2 November 2026 is the earliest hard deadline in the programme. And if you are negotiating an IP licence or an intra-group transfer, record the date the contract is entered into, because the Bill turns on that date rather than on your year of assessment.

Read the two separate things that happened this month

Two unrelated announcements have landed in the same fortnight, and reading them as one story will get both of them wrong. The Inland Revenue (Amendment) (Tax Deductions for Intellectual Property Expenditure) Bill 2026 was gazetted on 16 October 2026 and is a forward-looking change to the tax treatment of intellectual property.

The extended filing deadlines for 2025/26 returns are not a new announcement at all. They come from a Circular Letter the Commissioner of Inland Revenue issued to tax representatives on 19 March 2026, setting out the lodgement programme that has been running since April 2026.

The practical consequence is that a founder reading a summary of "this week's Hong Kong tax news" could easily believe that a deadline was moved last week, and that a new set of electronic filing dates has appeared. Neither is true. The scheme is seven months old, and the dates in it are set out in a single published table.

Key Takeaway: The IP Bill is a proposal, not law, and it applies only to contracts entered into on or after 1 April 2026. The 2025/26 return deadlines are not new: a Circular Letter issued on 19 March 2026 sets them out, and the paper deadline for an M code return is 16 November 2026.

If you are a loss-making company with an M code, apply for the extension to 1 February 2027 by 2 November 2026.

Understand what the IP Bill actually proposes

The Government published the Inland Revenue (Amendment) (Tax Deductions for Intellectual Property Expenditure) Bill 2026 on 16 October 2026. A spokesman for the Commerce and Economic Development Bureau framed it as putting into effect a measure announced in the 2025-26 and 2026-27 Budgets.

The measure relaxes the tax deduction arrangements for relevant capital expenditure incurred by enterprises for purchasing intellectual property rights or for the licence to use intellectual property rights. The stated ambition is to make Hong Kong a regional IP trading centre and an international innovation and technology centre, in alignment with the National 15th Five-Year Plan.

The point of the Bill is that Hong Kong already offers a preferential rate on qualifying intellectual property through the patent box initiative, and already has a foreign-sourced income exemption regime that covers foreign-sourced IP income.

What it did not have was a rule allowing you to deduct the capital cost of acquiring or licensing IP in the first place. The Bill fills that gap, and it does so in three distinct ways.

The three deductions the IP Bill 2026 proposes, and what each one is for
What is being deductedWho it helpsThe condition that governs it
Purchase of eligible IP rights from associates, such as group affiliatesMultinational groups that want to centralise IP management in Hong KongA main purpose test applies, together with a prescribed method for calculating the deduction on purchases from Hong Kong associates
Purchase of eligible IP rights used by a licensee outside Hong KongHong Kong licensors whose IP is used by foreign licenseesThe licensing income must be regarded as arising in or derived from Hong Kong under the foreign-sourced income exemption regime and be chargeable to profits tax in Hong Kong; the deduction is allowed on a proportionate basis
Upfront licence fee for the use of eligible IP rights in a licensing arrangementBusinesses commercialising eligible IP under a licensing structureThe fee must relate to the use of eligible IP rights in a licensing arrangement; the Bill gives statutory clarity for an upfront payment

The first limb is the one that will generate the most discussion. It allows a deduction for capital expenditure on buying IP from an affiliate, which is designed to make Hong Kong a more attractive place to hold group IP.

The second is narrower and technical: it addresses the case where a Hong Kong company licenses IP to a non-Hong Kong licensee, and the deduction for the purchase cost follows the licensing income that is taxable in Hong Kong.

The third is the simplest and arguably the most immediately useful, because upfront licence fees are a real cash cost that currently sits in a grey area.

All three limbs apply to contracts entered into on or after 1 April 2026. That date is retrospective to the start of the current financial year, which means a transaction already completed this year may already be in scope. It also means a transaction completed last year is not, and cannot be brought in scope by amending the accounting treatment afterwards.

Read the safeguards, because they are where the risk sits

The Department was explicit that the amendments come with a series of rigorous safeguard measures to safeguard the tax base and prevent abuse of the tax deduction arrangements. Three of them matter to a founder arranging a related-party IP deal.

  • A main purpose test for purchases from associates. This is the anti-abuse measure, and it is directed at the structure rather than at the price. If obtaining the tax benefit was one of the main purposes of the arrangement, the deduction is not available.
  • A prescribed calculation for capital expenditure on IP bought from Hong Kong associates. The deduction amount is not simply the price paid; it is calculated under a method set out in the Bill.
  • A third-party independent valuation report above a specified threshold. Where capital expenditure for buying IP from a non-Hong Kong business associate, or for acquiring a licence from an associate, exceeds a threshold, an independent valuation report is required to substantiate the claim.

Read together, these three safeguards say something specific about how the Government intends IP arrangements to be built: commercial substance, not tax outcome, is the test. A group that has been charging a nominal price for an intra-group IP transfer and claiming the deduction may find that arrangement no longer supports the claim it was designed to support.

Why this matters: The valuation threshold is the provision most likely to generate actual cost. A third-party independent valuation is a real professional fee, and a group with several affiliates in the chain may need more than one.

If a related-party IP transfer is material to your model, the cost of evidencing it belongs in your budget alongside the tax saving it produces.

The scheme works by accounting date code, and the Department published the outcome in a table of events for the lodgement programme in 2026-27.

The loss-case route is the one to act on first, because it has the earliest deadline in the entire programme. A list of file numbers is a small piece of work, and 2 November 2026 leaves less than a month from the date of this article.

The Department grants the priority to profit cases deliberately, so a loss-making filer that does not claim the loss-case extension is competing for capacity with profitable ones.

There is also a further extension to 1 February 2027 where a loss case with an M code is to be e-filed, and it does not replace the loss-case extension: it is the same final date reached by a different route.

The one-month trap in the other direction: The IR56F deadline for a departing employee runs backwards from the employee's departure date, while the business registration and employer's return clocks run forwards from the start of your business or your hiring. A founder who sees "one month" in two unrelated places and assumes they are the same deadline will miss one of them.

Check who the block extension actually reaches

The scheme is not open to everyone. It operates through tax representatives, and the Circular Letter is addressed to them.

The definition of tax representative in the Circular Letter is a person authorised by a taxpayer to act on their behalf for the purposes of the Inland Revenue Ordinance (Cap. 112), with the authorisation made in writing and bearing the taxpayer's signature. E-mail authorisation is not acceptable.

Any application for extension, including a block extension, must include a declaration confirming that written authorisation has been received from each taxpayer concerned. An application made without that confirmation is rejected and has to be resubmitted once the authorisation is obtained.

One consequence catches people out. Service providers engaged under section 51AAD of the Ordinance to furnish Profits Tax Returns for or on behalf of their clients cannot lodge block extension applications for them if they are not the tax representative defined under the Ordinance. Your incorporation agent or company secretary is not automatically able to file an extension request for you.

From April 2026 the scheme is electronic only. Tax representatives must submit block extension applications and notifications through the online block extension service in the Tax Representative Portal, and no paper submission is allowed.

The pre-registration requirement for the portal has been dispensed with, so a firm can access the service through a dedicated TRP Business Account without registering in advance. An acknowledgement receipt is provided immediately after a successful online submission.

If the firm has not yet registered a TRP Business Account, it needs to do so, and it can use a Business Tax Portal account for the client-facing e-filing itself.

Notifications of changes of client details have their own deadlines: 4 May 2026 for new clients appointed on or after 20 February 2026, for cases where the firm ceased to act, and for clients that changed their accounting dates, then 1 June 2026 for changes occurring between 4 and 31 May 2026.

Price the consequence of getting the date wrong

You now have a date to file by, so it is worth being precise about what happens if you miss it. The Department states that where returns are not lodged by the extended due date, estimated assessments will be issued or penalty proceedings instituted, and that taxpayers who have persistently filed late returns are almost certain to face penalty action.

The Circular Letter also makes clear that a claim for an extension that you then do not use is itself a risk: the further extension granted is regarded as void ab initio.

For a profits tax case that does not involve a field audit or investigation, the Department applies a published penalty loading scale for failure to notify chargeability to tax or failure to submit a tax return in time.

The Inland Revenue Department's section 82A penalty loading scale for profits tax cases, for failure to notify chargeability or failure to file in time
OffenceWhere the return is filed without an estimated assessment in betweenWhere two or more estimated assessments have been issued
First offence10% of the amount of tax undercharged20% of the amount of tax undercharged
Second offence within 5 years20% of the amount of tax undercharged30% of the amount of tax undercharged
Third or subsequent offences within 5 years35% of the amount of tax undercharged50% of the amount of tax undercharged

The Department notes that these percentages are for general guidance only and may be adjusted upwards or downwards depending on the circumstances of each case, with relevant factors including the length of the delay and the amount of tax undercharged.

It also notes that because section 82A makes no distinction between, among other things, understatement of income and late filing of a return, the exposure to treble the amount of tax undercharged applies to both.

A worked example makes the arithmetic concrete. A company with an M code files late in 2026, and the tax undercharged is assessed at HK$200,000.

On the first-offence line, with no estimated assessment issued in between, the section 82A loading is 10% of HK$200,000, or HK$20,000, on top of the tax itself. On the third or subsequent offence line, with two or more estimated assessments issued before the return arrives, the loading is 50% of HK$200,000, or HK$100,000.

Separately, and independently of the penalty loading, if you do not pay tax by the date specified in the notice of assessment, the Commissioner initiates recovery actions. These include the imposition of a 5% surcharge on the entire unpaid balance of the demand notice, the issue of a recovery notice to a third party, and the initiation of legal action, under Part XII of the Inland Revenue Ordinance (Cap. 112).

Section 71(1) of the Ordinance requires the tax to be paid in the manner directed in the notice of assessment and on or before the date specified in that notice. If the first instalment is not paid by that date, the second instalment becomes immediately due.

There are two more offences in the same Ordinance that a founder should know about, because they are the ones that turn a filing problem into a criminal one.

Section 80(1) is the prosecution provision for failing to comply with the return-furnishing requirements in section 51(1) and section 51AAD. The Department states that the offence may be compounded in lieu of prosecution under section 80(5), or dealt with by assessing additional tax in lieu of prosecution under section 82A where appropriate.

Section 80K is the equivalent provision for a service provider, covering a failure to furnish a tax return for or on behalf of a taxpayer and a failure to comply with a requirement under section 51AAD.

The record-keeping requirement is separately enforced. Section 80(1A) covers prosecution for failure to comply with the business records requirement under section 51C, and section 80(1) covers the rent records requirement under section 51D. Where a company is served through a service provider, section 80N allows the offence to be compounded in lieu of prosecution.

The practical distinction for a founder is between an administrative loading and a prosecution. A late return with tax undercharged is, in the Department's own framing, generally dealt with administratively by the imposition of monetary penalties in the form of additional tax under section 82A, and the published loading scale above is the starting point.

A prosecution under section 80 is a different order of consequence, and it is the reason the Circular Letter's warning about claiming an extension you do not use matters so much. The Department reserves the right to institute penalty proceedings, and it states that the further extension granted would be regarded as void ab initio.

Why this matters: The gap between a 10% and a 50% penalty loading on a HK$200,000 undercharge is HK$80,000, and it turns on whether estimated assessments were issued in the meantime. That is not a risk a founder can price after the fact.

It is why the loss-case application, which takes a list of departmental file numbers, is worth doing before 2 November 2026 even though the deadline it unlocks is 1 February 2027.

Work out which route is worth taking

The four routes are not equally valuable, and the arithmetic of the penalty loading makes it obvious which one a company with real tax at stake should be pursuing. A worked example makes the decision concrete.

Take a company with an M code accounting date whose 2025/26 return is due on 16 November 2026, and assume the tax undercharged is HK$200,000.

If the company simply misses the paper deadline and lets the Department issue an estimated assessment, the first-offence loading of 10% applies if the return arrives without an estimated assessment in between, and 20% applies if two or more estimated assessments have been issued. At 10% the loading is HK$20,000; at 20% it is HK$40,000.

If the company then repeats the failure inside five years, the second-offence lines apply, at 20% and 30%, so the loading moves to HK$40,000 or HK$60,000.

Now compare that with the administrative cost of doing the work properly. The voluntary e-filing extension costs nothing beyond a TRP Business Account and the e-filing itself, and it buys a full month.

The loss-case extension costs nothing beyond a list of departmental file numbers and a submission before 2 November 2026, and it buys until 1 February 2027. Set against a loading of HK$20,000 to HK$100,000, both are obviously worth doing, and neither requires a negotiation with anyone.

One point about the mechanism is worth flagging, because it is counter-intuitive. The loss-case extension is not simply the e-filing extension plus more time.

The Circular Letter states that it is intended that the lodgement programme should give priority to profit cases, and that a further extension to 1 February 2027 is available for M code clients who sustained allowable losses for the year of assessment 2025/26, whether or not the return is e-filed.

So a loss-making company can reach 1 February 2027 even if it files in paper, and it does so by taking a slot that would otherwise go to a profitable case.

The condition attached is a real one, and it is the thing to watch. The extension is granted on the condition that the Profits Tax Return to be lodged should show an allowable loss.

If you apply on a loss basis and then file a return showing assessable profits, the Department may institute penalty action under section 80 or section 82A if the request was made without reasonable excuse, and the further extension granted is regarded as void ab initio.

So the application is not a free option to be exercised later if the numbers turn out well. It is a statement about the outcome, made in advance, and it has to be true.

Track the six new double taxation agreements

On 14 October 2026 the Government announced that six Orders made under the Inland Revenue Ordinance would be gazetted to implement comprehensive double taxation agreements Hong Kong had signed with Barbados, Jordan, the Kyrgyz Republic, Maldives, Norway and Rwanda.

The Chief Executive in Council made the Orders on 6 October 2026, they were gazetted on 16 October 2026, and they were tabled at the Legislative Council for negative vetting on 21 October 2026.

None of these agreements is in force yet. Negative vetting is the mechanism by which the Legislative Council scrutinises an Order made in Council; if no objection is raised, the Order stands.

The agreements themselves will enter into force after both Hong Kong and the relevant jurisdiction have completed their respective ratification procedures, which is a separate step that does not follow automatically from the negative vetting outcome.

The Government's stated rationale was that the agreements ensure residents of Hong Kong and the relevant tax jurisdictions will not be subject to double taxation on the same income, and enable investors to assess more effectively the potential tax burden arising from their cross-border economic activities.

Hong Kong has now signed comprehensive double taxation agreements with 60 jurisdictions, 15 of which were signed by the current-term Government.

For a cross-border founder: A signed agreement that is not yet in force does not reduce your withholding tax today. If you are invoicing a customer in one of these six jurisdictions, the relief is not available until the agreement enters into force, and the double taxation protection is not retrospective. Factor that into any pricing decision that assumes treaty relief.

Work out how the IP Bill interacts with the exemption regime

One of the three limbs of the Bill cannot be read on its own, because it operates against the foreign-sourced income exemption regime. The condition is that the licensing income from the eligible IP rights is regarded as arising in or derived from Hong Kong under that regime and is chargeable to profits tax in Hong Kong.

If the income is not within the regime, the second limb of the Bill does not bite.

That regime still works through its own tests. Foreign-sourced interest, dividend and non-IP disposal gain received in Hong Kong by a member of a multinational enterprise group remains exempt if the economic substance requirement is met, with a participation requirement available as an alternative route for dividend and equity interest disposal gain.

Foreign-sourced qualifying IP income is governed by a nexus requirement, which works by reference to a nexus ratio modelled on the BEPS Action 5 Report and expressed through the R&D fraction. Only income from a qualifying IP asset qualifies, and the exemption is limited to the portion attributable to the qualifying expenditure.

So the sequence for a Hong Kong licensor with a non-Hong Kong licensee is this: establish that the licensing income is within the foreign-sourced income exemption regime and is chargeable to profits tax in Hong Kong, satisfy the relevant requirement, and then claim the proportionate deduction for the purchase cost of the IP. The Bill adds the third step to a regime that already has two.

One boundary is worth stating plainly, because it is a common source of confusion in a group that holds IP in one entity and licenses it from another.

A deduction claimed under the Bill is a claim under the Inland Revenue Ordinance (Cap. 112), and it is not an accounting entry. How the IP asset is recognised and carried in the accounts is a separate question governed by the applicable financial reporting standard, and the standards issued by the Hong Kong Institute of Certified Public Accountants set the accounting treatment.

A tax deduction you can claim in the return is not automatically a deductible expense in the profit and loss account, and the two should not be assumed to line up.

Plan the next four weeks in order

There are four dated actions between now and the end of November 2026, and they belong to different people.

  1. Confirm your real filing deadline. Ask your tax representative which accounting date code applies to your company and what the extended due date actually is. If the answer is 30 November for paper or 30 December for electronic lodgement, that answer is wrong; the paper deadline for an M code is 16 November 2026.
  2. Decide on the loss-case extension by early November. If your company sustained an allowable loss for 2025/26 and has an M code, the list of departmental file numbers must reach the Department by 2 November 2026. This is the earliest hard deadline in the programme.
  3. Date-stamp your IP contracts. If you have entered into a licence or an intra-group IP transfer this year, record the date it was entered into. The Bill applies to contracts entered into on or after 1 April 2026, and a contract signed in March 2026 is outside the scope of these amendments.
  4. Review your intra-group IP pricing against the safeguards. Check whether the main purpose test, the prescribed calculation and the third-party valuation threshold apply to any transfer in your group, and price in the cost of independent valuation if the threshold is exceeded.

What this does not do: Passing the Bill will not automatically make your IP deductions bigger, and it will not change the exemption regime's tests. It is a proposal until it is passed, and the Department will publish guidance on how the amendments operate. Nothing in this article changes what you must file for the 2025/26 year of assessment.

Read the frequently asked questions

What is the Inland Revenue (Amendment) (Tax Deductions for Intellectual Property Expenditure) Bill 2026?

It is a Bill that would let Hong Kong companies deduct more of the cost of buying and licensing intellectual property. The Government published the Bill on 16 October 2026. It relaxes the existing tax deduction arrangements for capital expenditure on IP rights and on licences to use IP rights, and it applies to contracts entered into on or after 1 April 2026.

First and Second Reading were scheduled for 28 October 2026. A Bill is a proposal, not law: nothing in it applies to you until it is passed, and the Inland Revenue Department will publish guidance on how the amendments operate.

What does the IP Bill let you deduct that you cannot deduct today?

Three things: buying IP from a group affiliate, buying IP that a non-Hong Kong licensee uses, and paying an upfront licence fee. The Bill proposes tax deductions for capital expenditure on the purchase of eligible IP rights from associates, for the purchase of eligible IP rights used by a licensee outside Hong Kong where the related licensing income is chargeable to profits tax in Hong Kong, and for upfront licence fees paid under a licensing arrangement.

Each of the three is new. The existing patent box regime addresses the profits arising from qualifying IP, not the capital cost of acquiring or licensing it.

Does the IP Bill apply to contracts your company signed before 1 April 2026?

No. The amendments apply only to contracts entered into on or after 1 April 2026. This is the single most important date in the Bill for a founder planning a transaction. Because the effective date is tied to the date of the contract rather than the date of the assessment, a licence agreement signed in March 2026 is outside the scope of these amendments, and one signed on 1 April 2026 is inside it. If you are negotiating a licence, the date on the signed contract may matter more than the accounting treatment.

What stops the IP deduction being used to avoid tax?

A main purpose test, a formula for the deduction amount on Hong Kong associate purchases, and an independent valuation for large related-party deals. The Inland Revenue Department describes a series of rigorous safeguard measures. For purchases from associates there is a main purpose test, so a deduction cannot be claimed where obtaining the tax benefit was one of the main purposes of the arrangement.

There is also a prescribed method for calculating the deduction on capital expenditure for IP bought from Hong Kong associates.

Where capital expenditure is incurred in buying IP from a non-Hong Kong associate, or in acquiring a licence from an associate, and the amount exceeds a specified threshold, a third-party independent valuation report is required to substantiate the claim.

What is the 2025/26 block extension scheme, and who does it apply to?

It is a scheme that moves the filing deadline for some 2025/26 returns, and it applies to taxpayers who have appointed a tax representative. The Commissioner of Inland Revenue issued a Circular Letter to tax representatives on 19 March 2026 setting out the arrangements for the 2025/26 lodgement programme. For taxpayers who are represented, the compliance date on the return is extended according to the accounting date code.

The Department also states that tax representatives must submit block extension applications electronically through the Tax Representative Portal from April 2026, with no paper submission allowed. A firm needs a Business Tax Portal account and a Tax Representative Portal account, and the circular sets out the registration and lodgement steps for each.

What is the filing deadline for a 2025/26 return with an M code accounting date?

16 November 2026 for a return filed in paper form, and you can get one further month on application if you file electronically. An M code accounting date is one that falls between 1 January 2026 and 31 March 2026. The block extension moves the paper filing deadline to 16 November 2026.

A further extension of one month is available on application where the return is e-filed voluntarily through the Business Tax Portal, counted from the later of 16 November 2026 or the normal due date of the e-return.

The application must reach the Department at least seven working days before the return would otherwise be due.

If my 2025/26 return is for a loss-making company with an M code, is there a later deadline?

Yes, and it is the most valuable date in the scheme, because you have to apply for it by 2 November 2026. The Department gives priority to profit cases. For M code clients that sustained allowable losses for the year of assessment 2025/26, a further extension to 1 February 2027 is available, and it applies whether the return is filed in paper or electronic form.

The application is made by providing a list showing the departmental file numbers of the cases, and the Department must receive it no later than 2 November 2026, with exceptional individual cases allowed on or before 16 November 2026.

The extension is granted on the condition that the return actually shows an allowable loss.

What happens if I claim an extension and then file a paper return or report a profit?

The Department may treat the extension as void from the beginning and may institute penalty action. Paragraph 21 of the Circular Letter is explicit. If an e-filing extension was requested and a paper return is subsequently filed, or if a loss-case extension was requested and the return filed shows assessable profits rather than an allowable loss, the Department would consider instituting penalty action under section 80 or section 82A of the Inland Revenue Ordinance if the request was made without reasonable excuse. In those circumstances the further extension granted is regarded as void ab initio.

Which six countries are covered by the new double taxation agreements, and when do they take effect?

Barbados, Jordan, the Kyrgyz Republic, Maldives, Norway and Rwanda, and they are not yet in force. The Chief Executive in Council made six Orders under the Inland Revenue Ordinance on 6 October 2026 to implement the comprehensive double taxation agreements Hong Kong signed with those six jurisdictions. The Orders were gazetted on 16 October 2026 and were tabled at the Legislative Council for negative vetting on 21 October 2026.

The agreements will enter into force only after both Hong Kong and the relevant jurisdiction have completed their respective ratification procedures.

Hong Kong has now signed comprehensive double taxation agreements with 60 jurisdictions, 15 of which were signed by the current-term Government.

Does the IP Bill change how the foreign-sourced income exemption works?

No. The Bill interacts with the foreign-sourced income exemption regime but does not change the regime's tests. One of the three limbs of the Bill concerns the purchase of eligible IP rights used by a licensee outside Hong Kong. Where the licensing income from those rights is regarded as arising in or derived from Hong Kong under the foreign-sourced income exemption regime and is chargeable to profits tax in Hong Kong, the capital expenditure on the purchase may be allowed for deduction on a proportionate basis.

The exemption regime itself still operates through its economic substance requirement, participation requirement and nexus requirement, which continue to apply to foreign-sourced qualifying IP income.

What should a founder do before 28 October 2026?

Three things: check whether you have relied on a deadline that is not the real one, look at the loss-case extension, and date-stamp your IP contracts. First, if you are a taxpayer represented by a tax practitioner, confirm the extended due date that actually applies to your accounting date code rather than assuming a paper deadline of 30 November or an electronic deadline of 30 December, because neither date exists in the Department's circular.

Second, if your company is loss-making with an M code, start the loss-case extension application now, because the 2 November 2026 deadline is the earliest in the programme.

Third, if you are negotiating a licence or an intra-group IP transfer, record the date the contract was entered into, because the Bill's effective date turns on that date and not on the year of assessment.

What does the IP Bill mean for a company that is not buying or licensing IP?

Nothing yet, but you should still read the safeguards, because they signal the direction of travel on related-party pricing. The amendments bite on capital expenditure on acquiring or licensing IP rights. A company with no such transactions is unaffected by the substance of the Bill. The main purpose test, the prescribed calculation for Hong Kong associate purchases and the third-party valuation requirement for large related-party deals are nonetheless a clear signal of how the Department and the Financial Services and the Treasury Bureau intend to police substance in intra-group IP arrangements, and they matter to any group that is already moving IP around, or plans to.

Is the tax treatment of an IP deduction the same as the accounting treatment?

No, and the two should not be assumed to line up. A deduction in the tax return is a claim made under the Inland Revenue Ordinance (Cap. 112). The accounting recognition of an IP asset, and the presentation of any government or incentive effect, follows the applicable financial reporting standard issued by the Hong Kong Institute of Certified Public Accountants.

A company claiming a deduction under the Bill should expect to be asked to reconcile the tax claim to the fixed asset and intangible asset register, and to produce the valuation report where the threshold is exceeded.

The tax rule and the accounting treatment are set by different bodies and do not move together.
